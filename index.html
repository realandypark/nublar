Methodology: how the numbers are calculated

Applies to the current version of the simulator. Last updated: 25 November 2026

Project Nublar turns the figures you enter into a year-by-year picture of a company's revenue, costs, tax, net income and cash. Every calculation is described on this page, and every input it uses is one you can see and edit. There is no hidden data, no weighting by probability and nothing that is not listed here.

1. Basic principles

  • Scope. The simulator models companies that develop or sell drugs. If a pasted AI answer says the company does not (for example a research-tools, diagnostics or device company), nothing is filled in and no results are shown.
  • Lite mode and Advanced mode. Lite mode is a simpler view of the same simulator: it uses the same research prompt, the same reading of the AI's answer and the same calculations, and only shows fewer figures at a time. Advanced mode shows and lets you edit every input. Both are free, and switching between them never changes a result.
  • Deterministic. You decide which drugs are assumed approved and which fail. The simulator never assigns a probability of success and never averages outcomes.
  • Yearly periods from today. Year 1 is the next 12 months from the day you open the page, year 2 the 12 months after that, and so on. Each period is labeled with the calendar year its midpoint falls in. These are not the company's fiscal years.
  • All money in millions of US dollars ($M), except price per patient, which is in dollars per patient per year.
  • No discounting. A dollar in year 10 counts the same as a dollar today. Totals are simple sums.
  • Length of the period shown. The simulation runs for the longest included drug's years to patent cliff plus its fade after the cliff, rounded up, plus one year, with a minimum of 6 years. It never runs longer than your Years to simulate setting (between 3 and 40).

3. A drug's sales

Peak sales ($M per year) = patients × peak penetration × price per patient ÷ 1,000,000

Sales follow a simple shape, measured as a share of peak sales:

  • Before launch: 0%.
  • Ramp: rises in a straight line from 0% at launch to 100% after the ramp to peak. A ramp of 0 means sales start at 100%.
  • Plateau: stays at 100% until the patent cliff.
  • Fade: falls in a straight line from the level reached at the patent cliff to 0% over the fade after cliff. A fade of 0 means sales stop at once. If the patent cliff comes during the ramp, the fade starts from the level reached at that point.
Sales in a year = peak sales × the average share of peak over that 12-month period

The average is calculated exactly over the period, so a launch partway through a year counts only the part of the year after launch.

4. Company revenue and product profit (if approved)

Company revenue from a drug = sales × company share of sales + approval milestones (in the launch year)
Product profit = sales × company share of sales × margin + approval milestones (in full) − yearly selling cost × the part of the year it is paid

The yearly selling cost is paid in full from launch to the patent cliff, because a sales force costs the same whether early sales are small or large. After the patent cliff it falls in a straight line to zero over the fade after cliff, in step with sales. If the patent cliff comes before launch there are no sales and no selling cost. This is why a drug the company sells itself usually loses money in its first years and earns more later.

Milestones are paid in the yearly period that contains the launch date (year 1 for a drug launching today).

5. R&D and launch spending (if approved)

  • Until the next catalyst (or launch, if that comes first): the drug spends its annual burn, but never more in total than its remaining R&D.
  • From the catalyst to launch: whatever is left of the remaining R&D is spread evenly.
  • If the catalyst falls at or after launch, the remaining R&D is spread evenly from today to launch. If launch is today (0 years), all remaining R&D is spent in year 1.
  • Pre-launch selling spend is spread evenly over the pre-launch selling period before launch, or from today if launch is sooner than that. With a period of 0, it is spent at launch.

Spending is calculated by the day and added up for each yearly period. "R&D still to spend" in the Drug by Drug table is the remaining R&D figure, without pre-launch selling.

6. If a drug fails

A failing drug earns no revenue and receives no milestone. It spends exactly as it would if approved up to its next catalyst (or launch, if that comes first), and nothing after that, including any pre-launch spend that would have fallen later. This is the cheapest way to fail: in reality a drug can also fail later, after more money has been spent.

7. Corporate overhead and wind-down

Overhead is charged each year while the company is still operating, prorated for a partial year. With wind-down set to Yes, the company operates until the latest of these points:

  • for each approved drug with a company share at or above the self-commercialized threshold: its patent cliff plus fade after cliff (when its sales reach zero);
  • for each approved drug below that threshold (royalty or minority share): its launch, because royalty income is treated as passive;
  • for each failing drug: its next catalyst (or launch, if earlier).

After that point overhead stops. With wind-down set to No, overhead is charged in every year shown.

8. Interest, tax and net income

Operating profit = sum of product profit − overhead − R&D and launch spending
Profit before tax = operating profit − interest expense
  • A loss pays no tax and is added to the tax-loss pool, which starts at the tax-loss carryforward.
  • A profit is first offset in full by the loss pool. Tax is the tax rate times whatever profit is left.
Net income = profit before tax − tax

9. Cash

Cash at the end of a year = cash today + the sum of net income up to that year

A cash balance below $0 means the company would need outside money (an equity raise, a loan or a partner) to carry on. The simulator does not add any such funding, and it does not model share issuance, working capital or timing differences between profit and cash. Debt is not an input: only its yearly interest (interest expense) is counted, and any repayment of debt is left out, so a company with debt falling due would have less cash than shown.

11. What the model leaves out

  • Any probability of success, risk adjustment or discounting for the time value of money.
  • Changes in price, inflation, competition, market growth or currency over time, beyond the shape of the sales curve.
  • Selling costs that move in other ways than a flat yearly amount from launch that falls with sales after the patent cliff, for example a sales force that grows as sales grow.
  • Limits on using tax losses (for example, the US rule allowing losses to offset only 80% of taxable income in a year): losses here offset profit in full.
  • Repayment or refinancing of debt, new share issues, dilution and the timing of cash versus profit.
  • Later-stage failures, delays and partial approvals: each drug is either approved on your timeline or fails at its next readout.
  • Anything about a company that is not entered as an input.

7. Known limitations of the model

The simulator simplifies how a biotech business works. In particular:

  • No discounting: a dollar in a future year counts the same as a dollar today.
  • A drug marked as failing stops spending at its next readout. Failing in a later phase would cost more, so failure scenarios show the lowest cost of failing.
  • Debt is not included: only the interest expense you enter is counted. Repaying or refinancing debt would reduce the cash shown. No new financing, share issuance or dilution is modeled.
  • Tax losses offset all taxable profit. Some countries limit this, so taxes in profitable years may be understated.
  • Revenue follows a simplified shape: a straight ramp to peak, flat until the patent cliff, then a straight fade. Prices, costs and market size do not change over time.

The methodology explains every calculation and lists the other simplifications.
